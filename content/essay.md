On August 2 2026, I found myself standing inside Madison Square Garden in New York City, watching J. Cole perform live.

Six years earlier, I was a sixteen-year-old boy growing up in Nepal, listening to his music for the very first time. Back then, the idea of seeing him perform in New York felt almost unimaginable. It was one of those things I might have dreamed about but never seriously expected to experience.

> And yet, there I was.

Of all the things I remember from that night, one of the most fascinating was the way Cole made his entrance. Rather than appearing with some extravagant introduction, he emerged right from the middle of the crowd. What struck me wasn't just the surprise of seeing him there, but how natural the moment felt. Nobody was desperately pulling at him or trying to drag him around. There was something remarkably calm and genuine about it.

[[pair crowd-entrance]]

Here was one of the biggest rappers in the world, walking among the very people who had come to see him.

I don't know why, but that moment stayed with me. Perhaps because it reflected something I had admired about him for years: the simplicity and humility that seem to exist alongside his extraordinary success.

But the most meaningful part of that night came later, when I heard him perform a song I had been listening to for years.

> "Love Yourz."

Standing there, listening to those familiar words in person, I couldn't help thinking about how much had changed since the first time I heard them.

And that is really what this story is about.

Not simply J. Cole, the rapper, but the person I've been becoming while growing up with his music.

[[timeline]]

## Everything Has Its Own Time {theme=paper layout=left}

I was introduced to J. Cole sometime around 2019 by my cousin.

I was around sixteen or seventeen, still figuring out who I was, what I wanted from life, and how the world worked. Like most teenagers, I enjoyed music, but I hadn't given much thought to how deeply an artist's words could influence the way someone sees things.

Then I started listening to Cole.

I remember being completely blown away. His storytelling, his honesty, the subjects he chose to explore, and the way he could turn ordinary human experiences into something so meaningful felt unlike anything I had heard before.

There was something incredibly real about his music.

He wasn't simply rapping about success, wealth, or being better than everyone else. He was talking about growing up, making mistakes, questioning himself, dealing with insecurities, chasing dreams, and trying to understand what really matters.

Even when I couldn't personally relate to the experiences he was describing, I could recognize the emotions behind them.

At first, I was almost disappointed that I hadn't discovered him earlier. How had this person been making such incredible music for so many years without me knowing about him?

I started going through his older projects, listening to his mixtapes, watching interviews, and trying to understand the person behind the music.

But eventually, I began to realize something.

> **Everything has its own time.**

Perhaps I discovered J. Cole at exactly the point in my life when I was supposed to.

I don't think the twelve-year-old version of me would have understood his music the way I did at sixteen. And looking back now, I realize that even at sixteen, there were things I wasn't ready to fully understand.

>> The interesting part is that the songs haven't changed. I have.

There are lyrics I barely noticed when I first heard them that mean something completely different to me today. Certain songs that I once enjoyed simply because they sounded good have become reminders of the kind of person I want to be.

I think that's one of the most beautiful things about music.

> Sometimes, we don't really understand a song until life gives us a reason to.

## A Dollar and a Dream {theme=paper layout=right}

One of the things I've always admired about Cole is his journey.

Before becoming the artist we know today, he was a young kid from Fayetteville, North Carolina, who loved music and had an enormous dream. He eventually moved to New York to attend college and pursue that dream, without knowing whether it would ever become reality.

His early career was filled with uncertainty and rejection. There's a well-known story of him waiting outside a studio hoping to give Jay-Z a CD of his music, only to be turned away.

But he continued working.

Years later, he became the first rapper signed to Jay-Z's Roc Nation label.

I find that story inspiring, not simply because of how successful Cole eventually became, but because of everything that happened before anybody knew his name.

The years of uncertainty. The disappointments. The decision to keep believing in something even when there was no guarantee it would work out.

Growing up in Nepal, I had dreams of my own. I wanted to experience the world, pursue an education, build a meaningful career, and create opportunities for myself that once seemed far away.

Eventually, I left Nepal to continue my education in the United States.

Of course, my journey is very different from Cole's. We grew up in different countries, under different circumstances, pursuing entirely different ambitions.

> But there's something universal about being young and believing in a future you cannot yet see.

I think that's why the idea of *a dollar and a dream* has always resonated with me.

It's not necessarily about becoming rich or famous. To me, it's about having the courage to pursue something meaningful, even when you don't know exactly how things will turn out.

And maybe that's why being in New York for his concert felt so special.

Years ago, Cole came to that city chasing a dream.

And years later, I found myself in that same city, far from the place where I grew up, watching him live out the very dream he once had.

That thought alone meant more to me than I can properly explain.

## The Person Behind the Music {theme=dusk layout=left}

I genuinely believe J. Cole is one of the greatest rappers alive.

His storytelling, lyricism, technical ability, and consistency make a strong case for that opinion. But I think what has kept me connected to his music for so long goes beyond how good he is at rapping.

It's the values I recognize in his work and the way he appears to approach life.

We live in a world where success is often measured by how much money we make, what we own, how many people recognize us, and how impressive our lives appear to others.

And I understand why those things are appealing.

I have ambitions too. I want to become successful. I want to accomplish things that make me proud and create a future that reflects the effort I've put into building it.

But Cole's music has made me question what success is really supposed to mean.

Despite his fame, he has generally chosen to keep much of his family and personal life private. He doesn't always seem interested in participating in the constant display of wealth, luxury, and attention that surrounds celebrity culture.

Of course, I don't personally know him, and I wouldn't pretend that everything we see of a public figure represents the entirety of who they are.

But I admire the values that repeatedly emerge through his music: humility, gratitude, honesty, family, and the importance of staying connected to yourself.

I admire the idea of being able to achieve extraordinary things without feeling the constant need to prove your worth to everyone else.

And that idea has become increasingly important to me as I've grown older.

As a student trying to figure out my future, it's easy to get caught up in what's next. The next opportunity, the next achievement, the next step toward the life I want.

Sometimes I become so focused on where I want to be that I forget to appreciate where I already am.

And no song has reminded me of that more than "Love Yourz."

## Learning to Love Yourz {theme=stage layout=center}

If there is one J. Cole song that has genuinely stayed with me through different stages of my life, it's "Love Yourz."

I've been listening to it for years and years, and somehow, it continues to become more meaningful.

Whenever I feel overwhelmed, disappointed, uncertain about the future, or find myself comparing my progress with someone else's, I often return to this song.

The message is simple, but understanding it and actually living by it are two very different things.

There will always be someone who seems to have more than you. Someone with a better job, more money, greater recognition, or a life that appears closer to the one you want.

And if your happiness depends entirely on reaching whatever level of success someone else has achieved, you'll probably spend your whole life chasing something that keeps moving further away.

I think about this a lot.

I'm at a stage in life where so much of my energy goes toward preparing for the future. I have goals, responsibilities, expectations, and things I want to accomplish.

Sometimes I convince myself that everything will feel better once I reach the next milestone.

Once I graduate. Once I find the right opportunity. Once I become financially comfortable. Once I achieve the things I've been working toward.

But then what?

There will always be something else to pursue.

And I don't want to spend my entire life waiting for some future version of happiness while overlooking the life I'm already living.

That's what "Love Yourz" means to me.

> It doesn't tell me to abandon my ambitions or stop wanting more from life. Instead, it reminds me that ambition and gratitude can exist together.

I can work toward a better future while still appreciating the present. I can admire someone else's accomplishments without believing that mine are somehow less meaningful. I can recognize what's missing from my life without forgetting everything I already have.

And perhaps most importantly, I can learn to appreciate the people, experiences, and ordinary moments that make life worth living.

The song hasn't magically solved my problems. I've listened to it during difficult times when nothing about my circumstances immediately changed.

But sometimes it changed how I looked at those circumstances.

And that made a difference.

[[scene live]]

I think that's why hearing "Love Yourz" live at Madison Square Garden meant so much to me.

For years, that song had been something I listened to privately, through headphones, while going about my life. It has accompanied me through different emotions, uncertainties, and moments of reflection.

And suddenly, I was hearing Cole perform it right in front of me.

I can't fully describe the feeling.

It was more than excitement about hearing a favorite song. It was the realization that something I had carried with me for so long was now becoming part of an experience I would remember for the rest of my life.

The younger version of me who first heard that song in Nepal could never have imagined being there.

And in that moment, the message of "Love Yourz" felt especially real.

I was so used to thinking about the things I still wanted to accomplish that I almost forgot to recognize how meaningful the present moment already was.

[[/scene]]

> For once, I didn't need to think about what came next.

>> I was simply happy to be there.

## More Than Just Music {theme=night layout=left}

Another thing I appreciate about Cole is the way his music has encouraged me to think beyond my own experiences.

Songs like "Apparently" made me reflect more deeply on family, sacrifice, and the people who support us while we're busy pursuing our ambitions.

"03' Adolescence" made me think about how easily we compare our lives with others without understanding the struggles they might be facing themselves.

And *4 Your Eyez Only* showed me just how powerful storytelling can be in helping us understand people whose lives are completely different from our own.

I grew up in Nepal, thousands of miles from Fayetteville, North Carolina. I cannot claim to have experienced everything Cole describes in his songs, especially the social and racial realities that shape many of his stories.

But I can listen, learn, and try to understand.

That's something his music has encouraged me to do, not just with songs, but with people.

To be less judgmental. To recognize that everybody has a story. To understand that someone's circumstances, mistakes, or achievements rarely tell us everything about who they are.

I also appreciate that Cole doesn't always portray himself as someone who has all the answers. He has publicly questioned his own decisions, admitted mistakes, and faced criticism.

I don't agree with every decision he's made or every message in his music, and I don't think being a fan requires me to.

But I respect the willingness to grow, reconsider, and learn.

There is something important about admiring another person without losing your ability to think for yourself.

And maybe that's what a meaningful role model should do.

Not teach us to become exactly like them, but encourage us to become better versions of ourselves.

## From Nepal to Madison Square Garden {theme=night layout=split}

Looking back at that night in New York, I think one of the most beautiful parts of the experience was realizing just how many different lives J. Cole's music had touched.

I met people from different places around the world, each with their own stories and reasons for being there.

Some had probably been listening to Cole long before I discovered him. Others might have found his music more recently. We had grown up in different places, experienced different things, and arrived at Madison Square Garden through entirely different journeys.

But somehow, we were all there for the same reason.

[[filmstrip night]]

There was something incredibly special about that.

I had spent years listening to an artist from a completely different part of the world, and now I was surrounded by people who had formed their own personal connections with those same songs.

It reminded me that music can bring together people who might otherwise never cross paths.

But more than anything, the concert made me reflect on my own journey.

I thought about the sixteen-year-old version of myself, sitting in my room in Nepal, listening to J. Cole because his cousin had introduced him to an artist he knew nothing about.

I thought about how excited I had been to discover his music, how much time I had spent listening to it, and how gradually those songs became part of my life.

And then I thought about everything that had happened between then and now.

Moving away from home. Growing older. Meeting new people. Facing new responsibilities. Learning things about myself that I couldn't have understood at sixteen.

I still have so much to figure out. There are dreams I haven't achieved, questions I haven't answered, and uncertainties about what comes next.

But standing inside Madison Square Garden reminded me that life has already taken me to places my younger self would have struggled to imagine.

>> Sometimes, when we're so focused on where we're going, we forget how far we've already come.

And perhaps that was one of the most meaningful lessons I took away from that night.

## Still Growing {theme=dawn layout=quiet}

When I first discovered J. Cole, I was drawn to his ambition, confidence, storytelling, and hunger to succeed.

I still admire those qualities.

But as I've grown older, I find myself connecting more deeply with the quieter ideas in his music.

Gratitude. Humility. Family. Self-awareness. Finding peace with yourself. Learning that success and happiness aren't necessarily the same thing.

I think that says as much about how I've changed as it does about the music itself.

At sixteen, I was fascinated by the idea of becoming successful.

Now, I'm beginning to think more seriously about the kind of person I want to be while pursuing that success.

I want to work hard without allowing ambition to consume everything else in my life. I want to accomplish meaningful things without measuring my worth entirely through achievements. I want to be someone who can admit mistakes, appreciate the people around me, and remain grounded regardless of what I accomplish.

I don't always succeed at those things.

I still compare myself to others sometimes. I still worry about the future, put unnecessary pressure on myself, and forget to appreciate the present.

But I think growing up is partly about learning the same lessons repeatedly, each time understanding them a little better.

And Cole's music has been there through much of that process.

I don't know what my life will look like seven years from now. Maybe I'll have accomplished some of the things I'm dreaming about today. Maybe my priorities will change completely. Maybe I'll discover that the things I thought mattered most weren't nearly as important as I imagined.

But I'm sure I'll return to some of these same songs, and I'm equally sure they'll mean something slightly different to me then.

>> Because the music may stay the same, but we don't.

When I think about that night at Madison Square Garden, I don't remember it simply as the night I finally saw my favorite rapper perform.

I remember it as a moment when two versions of my life seemed to meet.

The teenager in Nepal who had just discovered J. Cole, and the young adult standing in New York six years later, still figuring things out, but grateful for everything that had happened in between.

I once wished I had discovered his music sooner.

Now, I'm glad I found it when I did.

J. Cole didn't write my story. He doesn't know who I am, and I don't need him to.

But his music has accompanied me through enough chapters of my life that I'll always associate certain memories, lessons, and parts of growing up with his words.

And perhaps that's the greatest thing I can say about an artist.

Not that I want to live his life, or that I believe he has all the answers.

But that his music has helped me think more deeply about how I want to live mine.

I'm still chasing my dreams. I'm still figuring out what success means to me. And I'm still learning how to appreciate the life I have while working toward the life I hope to build.

Six years ago, I was a kid in Nepal discovering J. Cole for the first time.

Six years later, I was standing in Madison Square Garden listening to him sing "Love Yourz."

And for someone who has spent so much time thinking about the future, that night reminded me of something I don't want to forget:

[[closing]]

Sometimes, the life you're so busy trying to build is already giving you moments worth appreciating.
